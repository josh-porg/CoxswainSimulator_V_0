Why you are coding

Every call in the study was coded by a language model under the codebook. Your codes check that coding: for each field, how often a human who knows rowing and coxing reads the phrase the same way. That check is only worth something if your codes are your own, so the rules below keep anything but the words out of your view.

You will code 600 phrases from all fifteen pieces (yours and another coxswain's), in random order. Some phrases come round a second time, unannounced, to measure how consistent coding is. Then there is a short voice task.

Fairness rules

  • Code alone. Don't discuss sampled phrases with anyone (Claude included) until you have finished.
  • Words only. While coding, don't open the videos, transcripts, CoxBox files, results, the paper or the machine codes. You were in the boat for most pieces; code what the words do, not what you remember happening next.
  • Never use the outcome. Whether the boat sped up has nothing to do with how a call is coded.
  • Decide and move on. If two readings are close, choose the one you think fits the codebook better and press Shift+U (unsure). Don't agonise; the flag records it.
  • Take the breaks. Coding drifts when you are tired. Stop at the end of a block whenever you like.
  • Disagree freely. If your reading of a rule differs from what you think the machine would do, go with yours. Disagreements are what the check is for. A note (Shift+N) explaining your reasoning helps improve the codebook later.

The screen

The highlighted line is the phrase to code. The grey lines are the three phrases before it and the two after it in the transcript, there to show what it belongs to. Below are the eleven fields. The active field has a blue outline, and its definitions are shown underneath. The bar at the top shows progress and the block number.

Transcripts are automatic speech recognition from a microphone in a moving boat. Expect misheard words, sentences split across lines and run-ons. A phrase is whatever the transcript split off; it may be half a sentence.

Coding a phrase

  1. Target(s). Press the letter of each variable the phrase addresses. The first one you press is the main target (marked "main" when there are several); press a letter again to remove it. Press Space when done.
  2. The other ten fields. One letter each; the cursor moves on by itself. - or 0 means "does not apply".
  3. Flags and a note, if any (see keys).
  4. Enter saves and shows the next phrase. Check the row first: a saved phrase is locked.

If the only target is count or other, the other fields don't apply and are skipped: press Enter straight away.

Special cases

Several targets

When a phrase addresses more than one variable at once, choose all of them, the most important first. Only the main target is compared with the machine coder; the others are kept as extra information. Don't add targets just because they are loosely related; choose the ones the phrase is about.

Fragments

If the phrase only continues a call from the line before (the second half of a sentence, a trailing "all the way through"), code the call it belongs to and press Shift+F.

Signal words

Some words mainly shift the talk or call for attention ("okay", "listen", "alright"). The codebook has no category for that job. Code what the phrase itself addresses: if it addresses no variable, that is other; if you judge that it also addresses a variable, code that variable. Either way, press Shift+S (signal) when shifting the talk or calling for attention is part of what it does. A note on how it works is welcome.

Counts mixed with calls

A bare count ("one", "that's two", "ten more" used as a count) is count. When a phrase mixes a count with a call ("three, sit up, four"), code the call.

Numbers that are readouts

Splits, rates, distances and times read out ("2 .18", "you're at a 30", "500 to go") are reports, so info, not counts.

Misheard words

Code your best reading of what was meant and press Shift+X. If it can't be read at all, code other with the flag.

Blocks, resuming and locking

Phrases come in blocks of 40, about 15 minutes each, with a break screen between blocks. You can close the page at any time; next time it resumes at the first phrase not yet saved. Each saved phrase is written to a log in which every line carries a fingerprint of the one before, so a later change to any line would show, and a phrase can be saved only once. That is why there is no undo after Enter.

The voice task

When every phrase is coded, the page switches to the voice task: 220 short clips of calls, filtered so the words can't be made out while loudness, pitch and pace remain. Rate how aroused the voice sounds, from 1 (very calm) to 7 (very fired-up). Rate the voice, not what you guess was said. Space replays; a number rates and moves on. It takes about 15 minutes.

Afterwards

Tell Claude when you have finished. The agreement analysis was fixed in the codebook before you started: agreement per field between your codes and the machine coder's (and for arousal, the blind and lexical coders' too), how consistent you were on the repeated phrases, how often the machine's target is among yours, and, for the voice task, how your ratings line up with the acoustic measure and with each coder's arousal codes.
